Beat the Predictor

Tap left or right as randomly as you can. It will try to guess you.

About 2 minutes

Tap left or right 100 times, as randomly as you can.

How to play

Before every tap, a small program locks in a guess about which side you'll pick. You can't see the guess until you tap. Your goal is to be so random that it guesses right only about half the time, which is what blind guessing gets.

It learns from what you did after your last few taps. It knows nothing else about you.

On a computer, use F for left and J for right. To stop partway, hold the Quit button for one second, or press Esc twice.

The science behind this game

What it measures

This game measures how predictable you are when you try to be random. The main score is how often a simple program guessed your next tap. Blind guessing gets 50%. Anything reliably above 50% means you have habits the program can learn.

How the program works. It keeps a tally of what you tapped after each pattern of your last one, two, or three taps. It also tracks whether you tend to balance left and right. Before each tap it goes with whichever of these has been guessing best so far.

How this compares to the lab. Researchers usually describe human "random" sequences with statistics, such as how often people repeat or switch and how balanced their choices are [6]. This game reports two of those (your switch rate and longest streak) next to the program's score. One study used a statistical model to predict people's "random" choices and could predict about 60% of a sequence [7]. That study used a different model and typed 0s and 1s, so its number is a reference point, not a norm for this game.

What the research says

People switch too often. People trying to produce random sequences usually switch more often than chance would. They also judge sequences that switch too often as the most random [1].

We expect small samples to look balanced. People treat a short random sample as if it should look like the whole population [2]. A real coin often doesn't. It repeats and streaks more than people expect.

A long line of research. A major review covers how people produce and perceive randomness [3].

Even pros switch too much. Top tennis players mixed their serves well enough that they won about equally often on each side, as theory predicts. But even they switched direction too often [5].

Feedback can teach it. At first, every participant's sequences differed from random. After seeing feedback from statistical checks, they learned to make sequences those checks couldn't tell apart from a computer's [4].

Adult norms

Switch rate. Across the studies summarized in one review, people trying to be random switched about 58% to 63% of the time, compared with 50% for a truly random sequence [1]. That range is our summary of the studies in that review's table, not a single official norm. This game compares your switch rate with it.

Prediction score. No published norm exists for this program. The 60% figure above comes from a different method [7].

What's still debated

Why people make these patterns is not settled. One proposal is that people judge how random a sequence is by how hard it is to describe, so they avoid streaks that look too simple [1].

Limits of this game

One round is noisy. In our simulation of thousands of rounds by players with known habits, a single round's score landed within about ± points of that player's long-run average 95% of the time. Pooling 10 rounds narrows that to about ±4 points.

It reads a little low. The program starts each round knowing nothing about you, so it spends early taps learning. In our simulation it scored 1 to 5 points below the best possible prediction for players with simple habits. Against a real coin it scored exactly 50%, so a score above 50% always reflects real patterns.

Near 50% isn't proof of randomness. It only means this program, with its short memory, didn't find your pattern.

The simulation is a model. The player habits in it are our assumptions. Your own consistency, shown once you settle, is the better guide for you.
