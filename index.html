How it reads you

It only remembers patterns. The program doesn't know anything about you. It keeps a tally of what you tapped after every combination of your last few taps. Then it bets you'll do what you usually do after that combination.

Why that works. When people try to be random, they follow hidden rules. The most common one is switching too often. A real coin repeats itself all the time, so long streaks feel wrong to us and we break them up. The program picks up on that.

How to read your score. Guessing blindly, it would be right about half the time. The shaded area on the chart is where a coin's score lands 95 times out of 100. If your line ends above it, the program found real patterns in you, not luck.

What this can't tell you. One game of 100 taps is a small sample. The history table is more reliable than a single run. A score near 50% also doesn't prove you were random. It only means this program, with its short memory, couldn't find a pattern.

The science

  • People can't produce randomness on demand. A review of decades of studies found that people asked to make random sequences switch too often and avoid long streaks. Their sequences are too even to be real. Wagenaar, 1972
  • We expect small samples to look balanced. People believe a short run of coin flips should look like the long-run average, half heads and half tails. A real coin rarely cooperates. Tversky and Kahneman called this belief in the law of small numbers. Tversky and Kahneman, 1971
  • The gambler's fallacy is the same bias. After several reds in a row, people feel black is due. The wheel has no memory. Your tapping does, and that's what the program exploits.
  • The idea behind this game. Computer scientist Scott Aaronson wrote a short program like this one as a student. It counted what people typed after each five-key pattern and predicted most people well above chance. This game uses the same approach.
  • Even pros switch too much. Wimbledon servers mixed their serves well enough to win as often as theory predicts. But they still switched direction more often than a random server would. Walker and Wooders, 2001
  • Feedback can teach it. When people were shown how random their sequences were after each attempt, they learned to produce sequences that passed statistical tests. Without feedback, the bias stays. Neuringer, 1986
  • Why it matters. The same habits make human passwords guessable and make bluffs and play-calling predictable. Anything you try to vary on purpose, you tend to vary in a pattern.

What's still debated. Researchers disagree on why. One view is that we hold a wrong picture of what random looks like. Another is that our memory is too short to track a long sequence, so we balance the last few taps instead. Both likely play a part.
